One day, two teenagers strolled into Kennywood with two phones, two pockets, and a Physics toolbox app. The plan was simple: record everything, draw a chart. The data had other ideas: unfortunately, the GPS didn't quite work; the lattitude-longitude got somewhat affected by the pocket and wasn't the most successful. However, most of the linear acceleration and force data were quite accurate, and despite the altimeter losing seventy meters of drop, we decided to still put the data to use: somewhere in the middle of a 4.7g lateral burst on Sky Rocket the question stopped being what is the data and became what does the data feel like?

§ I

What the Body Knew Before the Brain Did

Sensory geography of a coaster ride

The brain processes a coaster ride about 100 ms after the body does. The vestibular system, in the inner ear, reports angular acceleration directly to motor neurons before any conscious thought catches up. By the time you've decided to scream, your body has already braced. The map below traces what each part of the body did, while you were busy reacting to what it did three steps ago.

Inner ear · vestibular system
First to know. Otolith organs detect linear acceleration; semicircular canals detect rotation. The lag between what your ears report and what your eyes confirm is the source of the spinning sensation that lingers after a loop. On Sky Rocket's inversion, the vestibular signal disagrees violently with the visual field for several seconds.
Stomach · enteric nervous system
The famous "stomach drop" is the abdominal viscera moving relative to the body cavity during sudden vertical acceleration changes. Phantom's 232-foot drop produces the canonical version of this sensation: the stomach lifts because everything else is falling slightly faster.
Neck · cervical spine
The most fragile articulation in the body, suddenly responsible for keeping a 5 kg head pointed where the eyes need it to be. Sky Rocket's lateral burst (peak jerk 136 m/s³) is what the lap bar can't protect against — the neck does its own bracing, instinctively, milliseconds before the force arrives.
Lungs · diaphragm
High vertical g compresses the diaphragm. Breathing becomes briefly harder. Most riders unconsciously hold their breath through high-g moments; the gasp at the bottom is partly relief, partly physiological need.
Eyes · visual cortex
During sustained high-g, blood drains from the head and the visual field begins to dim from the periphery inward. At 4.7g (Sky Rocket's peak), this is barely noticeable for a short burst, but the visual reduction is what airline pilots and fighter pilots train against. Coasters never sustain g long enough to be dangerous; they sustain it just long enough to be felt.

§ V

How the Data Becomes a Story

Pipeline · Math · Rendering
Each phone wrote a CSV of timestamps, three accelerometer axes, and four GPS columns at roughly 100 Hz. Turning that into the visualizations you've been looking at — and the ones in the rest of this volume — took ten steps. The GPS got dropped after the first one; everything you see is derived from the accelerometer.
STEP01

Recording

Phones in pockets running a sensor-logging app, sampling 3-axis linear acceleration at a nominal 100 Hz. The "linear" qualifier matters: this sensor reports acceleration with gravity already removed by the device's sensor-fusion algorithm. A phone sitting still on a table reads roughly $(0, 0, 0)$, not $(0, 0, 9.81)$.

The CSV also contained latitude, longitude, altitude, and speed from the phone's GPS, but the GPS receiver lost reliable lock the moment we boarded — Phantom's 232 ft drop registered as 1.5 m of altitude change. We discarded the GPS columns and built the entire analysis from the accelerometer.

STEP02

Ride window isolation

Each recording was several minutes long: queue, dispatch, ride, return to station. We had to find the ride. For each candidate window length $T$ (130 s for Phantom, 55 s for Sky Rocket — roughly each coaster's ride duration), we slid the window over the full recording and computed the total signal energy:

$$E(t_0, T) = \sum_{i:\, t_0 \le t_i \le t_0 + T} \lvert \vec{a}(t_i) \rvert^2$$

The window position $t_0^\star$ that maximizes $E$ is the loudest stretch of the recording. For Sky Rocket this lands cleanly on the actual ride; for Phantom the best window captured a still-noticeably-active 38 seconds of the recording.

Implementation: pandas.Series.rolling().sum() over the squared magnitude column, then idxmax().

STEP03

Force magnitude and jerk

For each sample, the magnitude of acceleration is the Euclidean norm of the three axes:

$$\lvert \vec{a}(t) \rvert = \sqrt{a_x(t)^2 + a_y(t)^2 + a_z(t)^2}$$

Converting to multiples of standard gravity ($g = 9.81 \text{ m/s}^2$) gives the number people actually use to describe coaster forces. A 4.7g reading means your effective weight at that moment is 4.7 times your normal weight.

Jerk is the time derivative of acceleration magnitude, computed by numerical differentiation after light smoothing to suppress sensor noise:

$$j(t) = \frac{d \lvert \vec{a} \rvert}{dt}, \qquad \lvert \vec{a} \rvert_{\text{smooth}}(t) = \text{rolling-mean}\,\bigl(\lvert \vec{a} \rvert,\ w\bigr)$$

where the window width is $w = f_s / 5 \approx 20$ samples (200 ms). Jerk is what your body actually braces against — a sustained 4g feels manageable, but the onset of 4g over 50 ms is what makes you yell.

Implementation: numpy.gradient() on the smoothed magnitude with the sample timestamps as the spacing argument.

STEP04

Event detection

Four event types are flagged automatically on each ride's timeline:

Peak force. The single sample where $\lvert \vec{a} \rvert$ is maximum.

Peak jerk. The sample where $|j(t)|$ is maximum — the most violent transition.

Airtime / low-force window. The longest contiguous run of samples satisfying $\lvert \vec{a} \rvert < 3 \text{ m/s}^2$. On Sky Rocket this captures the brief negative-g moment at the top hat; on Phantom it captures a longer smooth-cruise interval, which is why we label them differently.

Sustained high-g. The longest contiguous run satisfying $\lvert \vec{a} \rvert > \max(1.5\,\bar{a},\ 15 \text{ m/s}^2)$, where $\bar{a}$ is the mean magnitude over the ride window.

Contiguous runs are found by detecting transitions in the boolean mask and grouping samples between consecutive transitions.

STEP05

Velocity and displacement by numerical integration

Acceleration is the second derivative of position. Running calculus in reverse, we can integrate acceleration to recover velocity, then integrate velocity to recover displacement:

$$v(t) = v_0 + \int_0^t a(\tau)\,d\tau, \qquad x(t) = x_0 + \int_0^t v(\tau)\,d\tau$$

We use the trapezoidal rule for discrete samples: $v_i \approx v_{i-1} + \tfrac{1}{2}(a_{i-1} + a_i)(t_i - t_{i-1})$. The problem: any tiny constant bias in the accelerometer (every consumer sensor has one) gets integrated into a linear drift in velocity, and a quadratic drift in position. Over 55 seconds of recording, even a 0.05 m/s² bias produces a phantom velocity of nearly 3 m/s by the end.

To fight this, we apply a 2nd-order Butterworth high-pass filter with cutoff $f_c = 0.1$ Hz after each integration. This kills the slow drift while preserving the fast changes that correspond to real motion. The tradeoff: very low-frequency real motion (sustained acceleration over 10+ seconds) also gets attenuated. For Sky Rocket this works well. For Phantom, whose signal is already faint, the filter eats much of the real signal.

Implementation: numpy.cumsum() for integration, scipy.signal.butter() + scipy.signal.filtfilt() for the high-pass. filtfilt applies the filter forward and backward to eliminate phase shift.

STEP06

Kinetic energy and instantaneous power

Once we have velocity, energy follows. Assuming a representative rider mass $m = 70$ kg, kinetic energy is:

$$KE(t) = \tfrac{1}{2} m \lvert \vec{v}(t) \rvert^2$$

Power is the rate of energy transfer. By Newton's second law, the force on the rider is $\vec{F} = m\vec{a}$, and the instantaneous mechanical power delivered to (or absorbed from) the rider is:

$$P(t) = \vec{F}(t) \cdot \vec{v}(t) = m\,\vec{a}(t) \cdot \vec{v}(t)$$

For visualization we plot a scalar approximation $P(t) \approx m \cdot \lvert \vec{a}(t) \rvert \cdot \lvert \vec{v}(t) \rvert$, which upper-bounds the true power and reads cleanly. Sky Rocket's launch peaks near 34 kW per rider — roughly one-sixth of a Tesla Model 3's full motor output, delivered for a few seconds.

Implementation: pure NumPy, elementwise on the filtered velocity and original acceleration arrays.

STEP07

Frequency-domain analysis

Each coaster has a mechanical vibration signature: wheel chatter, structural flex, the dominant frequency of its layout events. To surface it, we detrend the magnitude signal (remove its mean), apply a Hann window to suppress spectral leakage from the finite recording length, and take the real FFT:

$$\hat{a}_k = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \bigl[\lvert \vec{a}(t_n) \rvert - \overline{\lvert \vec{a} \rvert}\bigr] \cdot w_n \cdot e^{-2\pi i k n / N}$$

where $w_n = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(1 - \cos\tfrac{2\pi n}{N-1}\bigr)$ is the Hann window. The bin frequencies are $f_k = k \cdot f_s / N$ with $f_s = 100 \text{ Hz}$. We plot $|\hat{a}_k|$ normalized to its own peak so the two coasters can be overlaid on the same axis even though their absolute force levels differ by a factor of five.

We display only the 0.1–15 Hz band because that's where coaster physics lives — track joints, structural oscillations, and ride events. Above 15 Hz the signal is dominated by sensor noise and high-frequency vibration that doesn't correspond to anything the body interprets as a force.

Implementation: scipy.signal.detrend(), scipy.signal.windows.hann(), scipy.fft.rfft().

STEP08

Spectrogram (time-frequency decomposition)

The FFT in § IX gives one spectrum for the whole ride — a single fingerprint. But the frequency content actually changes from moment to moment: the launch is one thing, the inversion is another, the brake run is silence. A spectrogram splits the signal into overlapping windows and runs a separate FFT on each, producing a 2D map of frequency vs. time:

$$S(t, f) = \left\lvert \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \bigl[\lvert \vec{a}(t_n) \rvert - \overline{\lvert \vec{a} \rvert}\bigr] \cdot w_n \cdot e^{-2\pi i f n / f_s} \right\rvert^2$$

computed over sliding windows of length $N = 256$ samples (2.56 s) with 75% overlap. We display $10 \log_{10} S$ — decibels — to compress the dynamic range so faint frequency content is visible alongside the loud bursts. Color encodes intensity: light cream for low, dark ink for the loudest moments.

This is the same technique audio engineers use to visualize music. Applied to coaster physics, it makes the launch on Sky Rocket appear as a vertical streak of low-frequency energy at a specific moment in time.

Implementation: scipy.signal.spectrogram() with a Hann window, then log-transformed and normalized for display.

STEP09

Statistical comparison

The histograms in § XI show that the two distributions differ. To quantify how much, we run a Kolmogorov-Smirnov two-sample test on the magnitude distributions. The KS statistic is the maximum vertical distance between the two empirical cumulative distribution functions:

$$D_{n,m} = \sup_x \lvert F_n(x) - G_m(x) \rvert$$

where $F_n$ and $G_m$ are the empirical CDFs of Sky Rocket's and Phantom's magnitude samples. The null hypothesis is that both samples come from the same underlying distribution; we report the test statistic $D$ and its p-value.

We also report Cohen's $d$ as a standardized effect size: $d = (\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2)/s_{\text{pooled}}$, where $s_{\text{pooled}}$ is the pooled standard deviation. By convention, $|d| > 0.8$ is "large," $|d| > 1.2$ is "very large." With thousands of samples per ride, any real difference will produce a vanishing p-value; the effect size tells you whether the difference is also big.

Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals on the means are computed by resampling each magnitude array 1,000 times with replacement and taking the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resulting bootstrap means.

Implementation: scipy.stats.ks_2samp() for the KS test, numpy.random.choice() for the bootstrap.

STEP10

Rendering

Everything you see on this page is hand-rolled SVG generated by vanilla JavaScript — no D3, no Chart.js, no plotting library. The HTML embeds a JSON payload (timestamps, accel components, magnitude, jerk, event annotations, integrated velocity, energy, power, FFT bins, spectrogram cells, and bootstrap statistics) computed once in Python and serialized at build time. The browser only does coordinate transforms, DOM updates, and pointer-event handling.

Timeline (§ VI). SVG path built by mapping $(t_i, \lvert \vec{a}(t_i)\rvert)$ to pixel coordinates via linear scales. The cursor and the body diagram are driven by a single shared variable, currentT; both re-render on every slider event or animation frame. Values between sample points are linearly interpolated so the cursor moves smoothly rather than snapping.

Body diagram (§ VI). A stick figure with three arrows whose lengths are proportional to the current $a_x$, $a_y$, $a_z$ values, clipped to a maximum pixel length. The magnitude ring around the body grows with $\lvert \vec{a} \rvert$. The plain-English description below is a lookup table keyed on the current $g$-load and jerk magnitude.

Velocity chart (§ VII). Two overlaid paths: naive integration (dashed) and high-pass-filtered integration (solid). A draggable vertical cursor reports the instantaneous values at any point in the ride. Cursor position is captured via pointermove events on an invisible overlay rect spanning the plot area.

Energy/power chart (§ VIII). Dual y-axis plot: kinetic energy on the left (gold), instantaneous power on the right (rust, dashed). Same draggable cursor mechanic. Stat cards below show peak and mean for both quantities.

FFT plot (§ IX). Two filled-area paths overlaid with transparency so the spectral overlap is visible. Normalized to each coaster's own peak so shape, not absolute amplitude, is what's compared. Cursor reports normalized amplitude at the hovered frequency.

Spectrogram (§ X). A grid of colored rectangles, one per (time-bin, frequency-bin) cell. Color maps linearly across a five-stop cold-to-warm gradient: ice blue → steel blue → violet → orange-red → deep red. Hovering reads out the frequency and time at the cursor.

Histograms (§ XI). Three side-by-side bars per bin for $a_x$, $a_y$, $a_z$. Bin count is 30 over a symmetric range chosen per coaster. Summary statistics (% time above 2g, RMS magnitude, dominant axis) are computed in JavaScript from the embedded series.

Statistics panel (§ XII). Pure HTML/CSS — no SVG. All KS, Cohen's d, and bootstrap values are precomputed in Python and injected into spans by ID at page load.

Body diagram (§ I). Static SVG illustration of a stylized human form with annotated body regions. Labels are positioned with absolute coordinates; lines are <line> elements connecting label anchors to body landmarks.

Equations. Rendered by MathJax 3 (loaded from CDN). Source remains LaTeX inside the HTML, so you can copy any equation back out as $...$.

Run the acceleration signal through a Fourier transform and each coaster's mechanical character emerges as a set of frequency peaks. Sky Rocket's spectrum concentrates almost all its energy below 1 Hz — its experience is dominated by a few big events (launch, drop, inversion) rather than continuous rattle.

Phantom's spectrum is broader, with secondary peaks scattered up through the 1–5 Hz band. Steel-on-steel wheel chatter, structural flex over a longer track, the gentler back-and-forth of an out-and-back design. The frequency content tells you what your body knows: Phantom is busier.

§ XII

Are the Two Rides Statistically Different?

KS test · Bootstrap CI · Cohen's d

"Different" is easy to assert from a chart. Defending it requires a hypothesis test. We treat each ride's magnitude samples as draws from an unknown distribution and ask: could both samples plausibly come from the same one? The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test compares the empirical CDFs; Cohen's d measures how big the gap is relative to the spread; bootstrap confidence intervals show how precisely we know each mean.

Honest note from the field

The GPS column in our recording is, quite frankly, garbage. Consumer phone GPS samples at 1 Hz with roughly 3-meter horizontal accuracy and worse altitude accuracy; a coaster drop happens in under two seconds. Pockets, metal track, and rapid orientation changes finished the job. Phantom's Revenge has a 232-foot first drop; our recording captured 1.5 meters of altitude change.

We considered hiding this and pretending the data was clean. Instead we built around the accelerometer, which was clean (3-axis linear acceleration at 100 Hz, ~9,300 ride samples between the two coasters), and we use Phantom's quieter signal honestly: an out-and-back hyper feels different from a launched coaster, and the data reflects it. The visualization above contains no fabrication.

Tools used: pandas, numpy, scipy.signal, scipy.fft, scipy.stats; vanilla JS + SVG for rendering.
